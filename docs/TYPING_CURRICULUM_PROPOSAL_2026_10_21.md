# Typing curriculum proposal: Grades 1–6

Status: historical curriculum proposal, retained after the owner-approved first release was implemented on 21 October 2026. The delivered subset is documented in [Typing first release](TYPING_IMPLEMENTATION_2026_10_21.md): Grades 1–6, US English QWERTY, 120 functional activity records per grade, expanded from sixty original unit seeds through twelve reusable roles. This is not 720 bespoke or independently piloted lessons. Typing routes, separate results and an optional validated private-course progress field now exist; existing coding curriculum and grading are unchanged.

Read the future-facing requirements below as proposal history, not a shipped feature list. Placement/beginner bridges, UK-layout selection, adaptive weak-key practice, spoken instructions, teacher-adjustable checkpoint goals, independently assignable typing courses, stored portfolios and dedicated trend/weak-key reports remain unimplemented. Student Typing currently follows existing assigned coding grades; raw writing stays in the session with explicit export. The implementation record governs present behavior and release limits.

## Course position and approach

- Typing and Coding are separate subjects on the courses page. Assigned typing courses would also appear independently on student dashboards, without pretending that typing is a coding lab.
- English QWERTY; the design sample shows US English. Choose the physical keyboard layout when setting up a class; UK and US symbol positions must not be silently conflated.
- Grade suggests content and reading level, not an inflexible starting point. Offer a short placement activity from Grade 2 upward, a beginner pathway at every grade, and teacher-selected starting units. Older beginners receive age-appropriate words, not infant content.
- Letters are available in the early grades. The progression changes independence, text complexity, editing and fluency, not merely access to keys.
- All passages, games and illustrations would be original or appropriately licensed. Edclub is a flow/pedagogy reference, not a source to clone lesson content, branding or assets.

## Proposed size and schedule

Expanded after owner feedback: each grade now has **10 units × 12 activities = 120 planned activities**, replacing the original 48-lesson outline. Across Grades 1–6 this is **720 planned activity slots**. This is a curriculum map and authoring scope, not a claim that 720 lessons have been written, reviewed or implemented.

Suggested delivery: around 30 teaching weeks, averaging four activities each week across two or three short sessions. Most activities are short screens; a session can contain several. Longer writing tasks may span sessions. Teachers may adjust this pace, and repeatable practice is additional practice, not another new lesson added to the published count.

Suggested session lengths: Grade 1 around 5–7 minutes; Grades 2–3 around 7–10 minutes; Grades 4–6 around 10–12 minutes. These are adjustable planning choices, not age norms or researched minimums. Each session may contain several very short screens, with a break whenever needed.

Each twelve-activity unit has the following mix. The numbered slots describe teaching roles, not identical exercises recycled between grades:

1. Meet the skill: concise demonstration and an optional spoken instruction.
2. Follow the hands: guided key or action practice.
3. Mix and match: combine the new skill with previously learned skills.
4. Recall and return: practise locating the key or action with adjustable guidance.
5. Read and type: age-appropriate copying using taught keys and vocabulary.
6. Use it: a meaningful word, sentence or practical text task.
7. Play with accuracy: a game without a compulsory countdown.
8. Story or puzzle: a different play-based application of the same objective.
9. Repair it: correct an error or revise a short piece of text.
10. Personal practice: a small teacher-selected or learner-selected review target.
11. Check independently: a short skill checkpoint with support use recorded separately.
12. Make something: a small applied task or portfolio item.

Per grade, that gives 40 guided activities, 30 applied-text/creation activities, 20 games or story puzzles, 10 repair/editing activities, 10 targeted reviews and 10 checkpoints. These categories are mutually exclusive for counting; a game is not counted again as a separate lesson or checkpoint.

The tables below give six concrete activity ideas per unit. Those examples develop the objective and variety; the twelve slots still require full prompts, passages, answer rules and accessibility review before implementation. Discovery, games, editing and independent tasks adapt to reading and motor readiness.

Variety must change the task, not merely its decoration. For example: a key-finding game asks for a displayed key; a word-building game asks the learner to type a word; a repair puzzle asks for an edit. All games need a clear typing objective and an equivalent reduced-motion, non-timed activity. Unknown letters may appear in instructions but are not required in scored practice until taught or deliberately introduced.

## Activity authoring and progression safeguards

- Every activity needs a stable proposed ID, a single primary typing objective, prerequisites, a text/key set, instructions, support options, completion evidence and a fallback for unavailable audio or motion. IDs are to be designed before code is written, not inferred from the display order.
- Sequence key coverage before word coverage: authors must check that required characters have been introduced. Guided teaching can deliberately introduce a new character; an independent check must not silently depend on an untaught one.
- Learning to type and knowing an answer are different tasks. Picture names, spelling, maths totals and subject facts must be supplied or supported when the objective is keyboard skill. Do not interpret an unfamiliar word or wrong maths answer as a mistyped key.
- A copied passage has an expected text. A composition may have many valid answers. Editing tasks judge the requested revision. Each needs its own success rule; a single exact-match or WPM formula cannot grade them all.
- Repeated key families must have a new demand: less prompting, longer or more meaningful text, a new context, deliberate correction or independent application. Do not relabel the same twelve screens to fill multiple grades.
- The hands show suggested finger placement; the app cannot establish which physical finger the child used from key events alone. Claims about technique need teacher observation or learner reflection, not fabricated automated detection.
- Beginner bridges, placement activities and optional coding-symbol extensions are separate from the 120 core activity slots. A placement attempt may recommend a starting point but does not silently award mastery for skipped work.
- Before scaling to all 720 slots, fully author and review one representative unit per grade, pilot the difficulty and session lengths, and revise the progression from observed needs. The expanded count is not a reason to release a large unreviewed bank.

## Assessment, progression and rewards

- Separate modes: **Learn** (guidance), **Practise** (repeat freely), **Check** (independent evidence), **Create/Edit** (meaningful application).
- Completion records participation; mastery records demonstrated skill. They are not interchangeable.
- No universal WPM pass gate. Show speed as private, secondary feedback when the sample is long enough and comparable; hide live speed for early learners by default. Do not score compositions, games or editing tasks as though they were copy tests.
- Candidate independent-check accuracy goals for classroom piloting: Grade 1 around 85%, Grade 2 90%, Grade 3 92%, Grades 4–6 95%. These are **proposed product defaults, not grade-level norms**; teachers must be able to adjust or waive them. Confirm them after a pilot before locking any progression rule.
- Track first-try accuracy separately from final text correctness. Backspace repairs text but does not erase the original mistype from first-try accuracy. Show corrections positively, with no loss of previously earned progress.
- A low result leads to short targeted practice, not a punitive reset or a speed demand. Reading difficulty, device/layout issues and access needs must be considered before interpreting a result.
- Preserve best achievements and history. Hints, retries and guide use do not remove previously earned rewards. Prefer unit milestones and personal improvements; no public leaderboard by default.
- Skill reports may show weak-key patterns, accuracy trends, comparable WPM trends and supported/independent checkpoints. Raw typed content should not be stored by default; avoid collecting real personal details in prompts.

## Access and interface requirements

- Guidance toggles: keyboard, finger labels, larger text and optional spoken instructions. No automatic audio or compulsory animation.
- Colour plus visible caret/underline/text communicates the next key and errors. No colour-only assessment information.
- Pause hides the activity; resume restores the place. Focused lesson input only, with normal Tab navigation; no document-wide keyboard capture.
- Physical keyboards are the primary device for touch-typing. Touchscreen exploration is a separate mode and must not be counted as evidence of physical-keyboard finger technique.
- Teacher-selected adaptations must allow alternative finger use, one-handed input and assistive technology without automatically classifying them as incorrect technique.
- Standard copy practice and free editing need different handling: ordinary pasted content is not proof of typing fluency; editing lessons may explicitly allow paste. Explain this without disabling useful platform access features indiscriminately.
- Localised content should be familiar to learners, with names and contexts varied respectfully. No real names, email addresses or passwords requested in practice text.

## Evidence informing the proposal

The grade structure, counts, activities, session lengths and provisional goals above are KodeArcade proposals, not a reproduction of another curriculum or a claim of standards alignment.

- [Edclub Grade 1 Typing Handbook](https://static.typingclub.com/m/edclubdocs/media/pdf/Grade1_Typing_Handbook.pdf): introduces all letter keys in Grade 1; discusses accuracy-first learning, short repeated practice, beginner corrections and accessibility. This supports introducing the alphabet early while adapting the amount of support.
- [Edclub Grade 3 Typing Handbook](https://static.typingclub.com/m/edclubdocs/media/pdf/Grade3_Typing_Handbook.pdf): includes keyboard rows, numbers/punctuation, cross-curricular text and placement options. This supports meaningful, cumulative applications rather than repeating identical introductory drills.
- [Typing.com explanation of WPM and accuracy](https://support.typing.com/en/articles/9048321): distinguishes speed and accuracy calculations. KodeArcade's eventual metric definitions still need an explicit specification and tests; the preview is not that implementation.
